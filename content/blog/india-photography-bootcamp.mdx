I trekked across India to practice photography.

# Photography Trek across India

For the past 10 days, I joined a photography workshop that took us across four cities in India. The first few days were rough: new people, long travel days, and constant 5am wakeups that made me question everything. But between the cramped autos and hours spent chasing light through unfamiliar streets, I was growing. Ten days of practicing a single craft, surrounded by people much better than me, forced me to pay attention in a way I hadn’t before.

At first, I remember telling my parents I might be wasting my time. My friends couldn’t understand why I’d spend that much money traveling around India, just to practice something they saw as a hobby. But these ten days ended up being transformative beyond just photography, but in how I see stories, people, and in the small details I used to miss.

This is my reflection on the journey.

## Chennai: Ordinary Moments

The trip started slow in Chennai. The day we all got to the hotel, we toured a famous bazaar in Chennai. The goal was to get our feet wet with street photography, learning to pay attention to small moments around us. This was extremely needed for me since my usual way of exploring cities is going in with a specific goal, recording myself while accomplishing said goal, and maybe taking a couple of cool landscape pictures along the way.

At the market, I had no idea what I was doing or what I could take pictures of. I even started with videos, awkwardly, since I barely knew how to use my camera settings. I saw Jacob, another participant, going crazy taking portraits of every interesting-looking subject, while I slugged around reservedly. But as we explored, the team encouraged me to hold my shots, look for interesting people, and just vibe with the surroundings. It felt weird, but clearly the people I was shooting with were in a different world, and I had a lot to gain by observing what they were trying to teach me.

The second day was even more ordinary. We woke up at 5 am to go to a fish market. My brain was in shambles trying to make sense of it all, "I don’t eat fish... what was I doing at a fish market?!" The overcast weather added to my apprehension, but after walking around the market, it was obvious how many hidden stories there were. What started with pictures of fish and vendors turned into an impromptu tour of the boats! Apparently, if you ask with a smile, these fishermen are happy to take you aboard, show how they prepare the fish, even how they clean the boat with acid.

Next stop was the flower market, equally mundane yet full of stories. I wondered how many years these people must have continued their routines, how intimately they must know the rhythms of the market.

After lunch and a short break at the hotel, we went to the beach. I didn’t understand why we were there on an overcast day, not even planning to swim. But our challenge was finding stories even in the mundane. There were old men untangling their nets, sitting stoically on their boats as I angled my camera at them. We infiltrated a group of kids playing cricket, and sniped pictures of vendors frying corn while fiery sparks flew into the rainy air. I never would have imagined these sidequests were possible, but somehow, walk in with an open mind, and they appear in your life. The rain cleared up right before we left, and we ended with a silly picture of us under a double rainbow.

I didn’t meaningfully improve my photography skills yet, but at least I was more aware of the world of stories around me that, in my ego and fear, I used to miss.

## Thiruchendur: Overwhelmed by Chaos

Our next city was Thiruchendur, a well-known temple by the sea in Tamil Nadu, during the Kulasai festival for Dusshera. I wasn’t too happy about it, since I had already visited Thiruchendur temple a few weeks prior with a couple friends. But I was comically wrong—this was a completely different experience.

Reaching Thiruchendur was its own side quest, being such a remote destination. We landed at an airport four hours away and boarded what was one of the most uncomfortable yet entertaining buses I’ve ever been on. Herbie, you will never be forgotten. And being that remote, the food situation was, let’s say, limited. We survived on dosas, pizzas, and candied peanuts. Not the worst food, but the lack of protein was legendary.

The highlight was the Kulasai festival, a gathering of tens of thousands of people across Tamil Nadu dressed up in traditional costumes as deities to celebrate Dusshera. This tradition has been going on for what seemed like hundreds of years, with each household sending their male members while women watched with enthusiasm. These men wore elaborate saris and makeup, which in many ways honored women. Everyone gathers on the beach at night to participate in elaborate fire rituals, filled with a kind of possessed energy.

As with any ancient event in the modern world, the place was flooded with photographers. Sometimes it felt like there were more photographers than participants. It felt wrong. Like a bastardization of the festival. Instead of living in the moment, people were there to extract content. Maybe that was my own insecurity masked as moral high ground, but I felt out of place.

The participants, however, were unfazed, and that made me feel even more conflicted. Some even invited us into their homes to see how they got ready and performed poojas. Watching others shoot so freely, I realized how focused they were on their vision. They didn’t let awkwardness or confusion distract them from their goals. It took me most of Thiruchendur to come to terms with my shakiness and self-doubt in terms of my goals.

My lack of technical skills didn’t help either. I was still trying to take landscapes, attemping to make sense of ISO, aperture, and shutter speed. The group pushed me to get closer and get more details, maybe try foreground elements, but still felt extremely out of place. Discovering the light meter helped a bit, but when the sun went, I relied heavily on my group to help figure out shot making.

In the end, I did get a few good pictures. The settings, the discomfort, and the idea of living in the moment were starting to make sense. It’s still fascinating how many different stories can exist in such a small area—the many sides of life you’d never realize you wanted to experience.

## Varanasi: The Breakthrough

With the chaos of Thiruchendur behind us, we made our way to Varanasi. The first half of the trip was meant to experience what it means to be a photographer, but now, it was time to learn how to be one. The colors and vastness of Varanasi turned out to be the perfect backdrop for practicing to notice small details, and finally learning how to work my camera.

The mornings started early. We drove through flooded streets to the muddied banks of the Ganga. Varanasi itself was indescribably dynamic. We took a scenic boat tour down the numerous ghats—these iconic steps and structures that define the riverfront, and gives context for how powerful the Ganga really is. However they managed to build such solid structures along such a fluid river, I will never understand.

The streets were filled with chai wallahs and people bathing in the river, separated by narrow alleyways, colorful steps, temples, monkeys, and dogs. And the colors—reds and oranges everywhere—added such an energetic flair to the city, it was impossible to feel tired despite by 4 hours of sleep a day. The nights were just as active. Huge crowds gathered around the pyres for the evening blue hour aartis, huddling under umbrellas to show their devotion even while being mercilessly rained on.

All of this was the perfect backdrop for my real training to begin. We started with the basics: finding subjects, framing them correctly, focusing on the light. Creativity showed up with placing them in front of matching backgrounds or aligning them subtle leading lines. I exercised my eye with with small exercises like finding five pairs of hands, and hilarious lining up four bald heads in a single picture. I was finally starting to get comfortabling playing around with my surroundings, without having to achieve anything immediate.

The pinnacle came when we found this intense-looking old lady during a night aarti. She agreed to post in a specific spot and let us take her picture with the aarti lamp we handed her. I got a mix of portrait, bottom, top, landscape photos, with a ghat as a backdrop. It became one of my favorite shots—her eyes fierce, her flame ethereral, the ghat standing menacing.

Before this trip, I never expanded out of landscapes or tripod shots of myself. Now, shooting 200 photos per hour in a single locatin started to feel natural. I could relive the story through the frames I captured.

## Kolkata: Dialing It In

Kolkata was our final stop. It seems like an ordinary metropolitan city: similar waterfronts to Varanasi, similar bazaars to Chennai, similar food to Bangalore. But this was my learning ground where everything started to click.

Speaking of grounds, on the second day, I got into a fight. We were photographing traditional Indian wrestlers, and I engross myself in the scene as much as possible. Noticing how they all walked around barefoot, I took my shoes off as well. They also noticed and got me to try a couple of their exercises, and once I did way better than expected, suddenly I was thrown into the ring. I survived for a respectable 2 minutes before getting absolutely pummled, but what an experience. I realized I liked mirroring my subjects and entering their world: being in the scene helped me take more natural, personal photos.

We explored a college-town book bazaar with gorgeous blue-painted walls and stacks of books that made for perfect frames. My mentor pushed me to keep getting closer, to eliminate negative space and focus on the story within the frame. Every time I thought I was close enough, he told me to get closer still: intention was everything.

By this point, I was moving lighter, crouching easily, and framing faster. My friend let me try his Leica Q3, and the fixed focal length forced me to move for every shot. It made me more intentional, and probably is going to be my next birthday present. Can't overstate how much Kolkata was useful in hammering in my technique and style.

# 10 Days, Poof

If I were a police officer of the universe, I’d make it a crime how fast time flies. In the moment, you resent the boredom and fall into doomscrolling, but on the flight back, you just regret not savoring each second. Ten days disappeared in a blink.

## What I Came In Knowing (and Not Knowing)

My relationship with photography is complicated. As a kid, I hated even getting my photo taken—maybe because I didn’t like how I looked or thought photos were useless, since the moments would seemingly always be there. Parts of my thoughtprocess I've now overcome. Later, on a trip to Alaska, I tried vlogging (thanks, Casey Neistat) and realized I actually enjoyed taking photos. On later trips, I loved photographing my parents, and when they couldn’t take good photos of me, I’d use my tripod and clicker.

But over the past year, I realized my photos weren’t as good as I remembered. My one friend could always find something I could improve—framing, subject placement, angle—and I agreed As I met more creatives, tried making videos myself, I realized there was a deeper level of intentional story telling I had yet to unlock. This workshop came at the perfect time. A trip around India to strengthen skills I'd been working on, while I was already in India? A sign from God to broaden my horizons for sure.

## What I Actually Learned

I could say I learned exposure settings, or the basics of Lightroom, or even how to use a Leica. But what I really learned was observation.

I see framing differently now. I used to love landscapes, but now I appreciate how subjects make stories feel like they're being experience through photos. Maybe you center them between walls, use leading lines, add depth with a foreground. Or when no subject stands out, you can chase light and shadow to find one. And turning off the editor brain that makes you feel a shot might be bad, and instead, rapid fire getting 4-5 shots, and moving on to the next subject.

I’ve also learned that making people comfortable is half the art. If my style means approaching strangers, I need to act like I belong there. Mirroring curiosity makes others feel curious too, and that’s when you get the best shot. And of course, it lets me take better creative risks, like getting closer.

Finally, photography is about capturing action in a still. Its about noticing when a street vendor is preparing a dish for a customer, and waiting for the exact moment when its handed over. Or capturing the beads of sweat of a shop keeper repainting his store sign. A good shot is awareness and patience combined—anticipating where a story might unfold.

From day 1 to day 10, my mindset is completely different. Graduating beyond landscapes and selfies, I now try to notice the small, fleeting moments around me. And when I see others’ photos, I can’t help but spot wasted negative space or awkward framing—not to judge, but to learn. It's still difficult nailing my own unique shots, but half the battle is to just keep shooting.

## Photography as Practice

Pursuing any art is deeply introspective. Becoming better means overcoming insecurities, ego, selfishness and the desire for recognition. It means doing the work for the sake of the work itself. A classic Bhagvad Gita lesson, comical how often this feels like the core lesson I need to learn in life.

There were moments on this trip where I felt flow. This one afternoon, we spent time circling an intersection, camera in hand, noticing everything. For a few minutes, as I pour all my attention into finding the next face, I forgot about my internal thoughts. I assume that’s what real pursuance ofart feels like, letting your body and instincts take over.

Finally, improving my art comes from consistent inspiration & output. On the inspiration side, I need to look at a LOT more photographers' work and see how I can mirror them to start. They've done a lot of the hard work already, so I can save years of honing my own style by learning from them. At the same time, I need to keep putting out my work. Instead of sitting on unpublished projects for years, even though I give myself time to finish, there should be a somewhat consistent candence of output. Again, shouldn't be burning myself out, but its better than a mountain of unfinsihed, more gstress inducing work.

## The group

Anyone can pick up a camera and travel across India, but the group made this trip. The jokes, the quirks, the habits—it’s funny how much you learn about people when you travel intensely together for a single purpose.

Greatful to Gajan and Shivam for making the trip so smooth and educational. I met Gajan on a cruise to Antarctica and was impressed by his eye for detail. His “cut the negative space” and “get closer” will forever haunt me—in a good way. Shivam was the most competent fixer I’ve ever met. And shoutout to Jacob and Kevin—learned a lot about style and technique from the boys. Couldn’t have asked for a better crew.

# Conclusion

Overall, I’m extremely grateful for this experience. I’ve learned and grown a lot in mindset, weird for a photography trip, but they're more linked than I ever thought. I still feel like an chump compared to the greats of photography, but that’s life, there’s always more to learn.

I want to keep photography as an art and passion, ideally not something I stress myself out over.
