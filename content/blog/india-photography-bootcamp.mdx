I trekked across India to practice photography.

# Photography Trek across India

For the past 10 days, I joined a photography workshop that took us across four cities in India. I had no idea what to expect. It was expensive, uncomfortable, and honestly, I wasn’t sure if I had made a mistake. I remember telling my parents I might be wasting my time. My friends couldn’t understand why I’d spend that much money traveling around India, a “cheap” country, just to practice something they saw as a hobby.

But I’d already committed, and there was no going back. The first few days were rough: new people, long travel days, and the constant 5am wakeups that made me question my life. But somewhere between the flights, cramped autos, and hours spent chasing light through unfamiliar streets, I started noticing how much I was changing. Ten days of practicing a single craft, surrounded by people much better than me, forced me to pay attention in a way I hadn’t before.

Those ten days ended up being transformative beyond just photography, but in how I see stories, people, and the small details I used to miss.

## Chennai: Ordinary Moments

We started slow in Chennai. The day we all got to the hotel, we started by going to Parry's corner, a famous bazaar in Chennai. I believe the goal was to get our feet wet with street photography, learning to pay attention to the small moments around us. Which was EXTREMELY needed for me, since usually, my way of exploring cities is going with a specific goal, recording myself while accomplishing said goal, and maybe taking a couple cool landscape pictures while I'm at it.

At the market, I had no idea what I was doing or what I could take pictures of. I even started with videos, awkwardly, I must say, since I barely knew how to even use my camera settings. I saw the other participant, Jacob, going crazy taking portraits of every cool looking subject he saw, while I was walking around reservedly. But as we walked, I was encouraged to hold my shots, look for cool looking people, and vibe to the surroundings. I can't stress how weird it all was, but clearly, the people around me were in a different world, and I had a lot to gain by observing what they were trying to teach me.

The second day was even more ordinary. We woke up at 5 am to go to a fish market. What was I doing at a fish market, even though I don't eat any fish!?! The overcast weather added to my apphrension, but after walking around the market, it was obvious how many hidden stories there were around me. What started with taking pictures of the various kinds of fish, the vendors, the boats, turned into a literal tour of the boats! Apparently, if you ask with a smile, these fishermen are more than happy to take you aboard, how thye clena the fish, even how they clean the boat with acid. Next stop was the flower market, equally mundane, but equally filled with the stories of people selling their products. I wonder how many years they must have continued their routines, how intimately they must know the dynamics of the market.

A lunch and a break at the hotel, we went to the beach. Insert "but we have a beach at home" meme here, since I'm not skilled enough at English to describe my confusion about why we went to a beach without intending to swim. What possible lessons could we have learned at the beach on an overcast, rainy day? Yet, the challenge was finding the hidden stories around us. There were these focused old men untangling their nets and sitting all stoic on boats, unfazed, where I put my camera up to their faces. We invaded a group of kids playing cricket, and even joined in on a couple of balls. We spied these vendors frying corn on the cob, while we got these crazy pictures of sparks of fire flying out as a foreground to a carnival on the beach. The rain cleared up right before we headed home, and we took a silly picture of us under a double rainbow.

I didn't meaningfully improve my photography skills yet, but atleast I was more aware of the wealth of stories around me, that in my ego and fear, I was blind to.

## Thiruchendur: Overwhelmed by Chaos

Our next city was Thiruchendur, a well known temple by the sea in Tamil Nadu, for Dusshera, or what they call the Kulasai festival. I wasn't too happy about this, since I already visited what I thought was Thiruchendur temple a couple weeks prior. But it was comical how wrong my preconceived notions were, since we didn't go near even 1 mile close to the temple I visited.

Reaching Thiruchendur was its own sidequest, being such a remote destination. We landed at an airport 4 hours away, and boarded what was one of the most uncomfortable yet entertaining buses I've ever been on. Herbie, you will never be forgotten. And being a remote destination, you can imagine the food situation yourself. We survived on dosas, pizzas, and these candied peanut snacks. Not the worst-tasting food, but the lack of protein was legendary.

The highlight of Thiruchendur was the Kulasai festival, a gathering of tens of thousands of people across Tamil Nadu, who are dressed up in traditional costumes as dieties, to celebrate Dusshera. This is a tradition going on for what seemed like hundreds of years, with each household sending usually their male members, while the women watched with enthusiasm. It was not a gender discrimination issue, as these men wore elaborate saris and makeup, which, if anything, honored women. At night, everyone gathers at the beach, with people going through elaborate rituals with fire and what seemed like possessed energy. I don't want to talk about traditions I don't understand, but that is what it looked like to me.

As with any arcane event in the modern world, the area and festival was FILLED with photographers and videographers. At certain points, it felt like the beaches and villages had more photographers than participants. To me, it felt like a bastardization of the festival. Instead of living in the moment, these photographers seemed as though they came here purely to extract content for themselves. Perhaps it was a holier-than-thou emotion that I was elicited to mask my skill issues around taking good pictures, but I felt extremely out of place, and lacked the will to take photos.

My awkwardness was exacerbated, since the participants themselves were unfazed by the photos. Some even invited us into their homes to let us see how they get ready, and the poojas (prayers) they conduct while getting ready. Everyone else around me taking photos, clearly came with a purpose, a vision and experience they wanted to capture. They didn't let awkwardness and confusion in the moment distract them from the original goal they set for themselves. They didn't let themselves get diverted with self doubt masked as "morals". It took a majority of the time in Thiruchendur for me to understand and come to terms with my shakiness in goals.

My lack of skills at this point didn't help either. I was still stuck in the mode of trying to capture landscapes and the entire scene. That by itself is fine, but it again masked my discomfort in getting upclose to these participants and capturing portraits, or using props and fire pits as interesting foreground elements. I was still stuck on trying to make sense of it all, instead of getting the shots and piecing them together later. Iso and aperture, and shutter speed still barely made any sense to me. Discovering the light meter slightly helped, but especially when the light began to fade, I started HEAVILY relying on my teachers to get the right settings.

In the end, I did get up getting a couple cool shots. The settings, the discomfort, the living in the moment are slowly starting to make sense. It will forever be fascinating how many different stories exist in such a small area; the many sides to life that you would never even realize you want to experience.

## Varanasi: The Breakthrough

With the chaos of Thiruchendur winding down, we made our way to Varanasi. The first half of the trip was meant for me to experience what it's like to be a photographer, but now, it was time to learn HOW to be a photographer. The colors and vastness of Varnasi, turned out to be the perfect backdrop for this concertedly practing noticing the small details and learning to work my camera.

The mornings started early. We drove through the flooded streets to the equally muddied banks of the Ganga. Varanasi itself was undesciabbly dynamic. We took this scenic boat tour down the numerous Ghats, these specific spots of steps and groups of structures, which contextualized the vastness of the city. How did they ever build up these solid structures on the banks of such a powerful river? The streets filled with Chai wallas and people taking dips in the ganga, were separated by these narrow alleyways, colorful steps, and temples, with, of course, monkeys and dogs (poor things) coexisting with humans. And the color. I wondered if there was a discussion behind the scenes, if there was a requirement for buildings to be colorful. The reds and oranges added such an energetic flair to the city, it was hard to ever feel tired.

The nights were just as energetic. Huge crowds gathered around the pyres, where they participated in the blue hour aartis. I'm not sure what they represent, but my guess is devotion towards god, which was abundantly clear as people huddled under umbrellas to stay for the entire aarti even as they were mericilessly rained on.

All these elements were the perfect backdrop for my training to truly begin. We started with the basics, like finding subjects that interested us, and framing them correctly. Framing includes lining them up with colorful buildings or door frames, or maybe finding leading lines that draw the eyes to the subject. I experimented with foreground elements, perhaps I could line up a fire with a sage in the background, the depth of the picture telling a more detailed story. With tasks like finding 5 sets of hands or 4 bald heads, I was finally getting comfortable with just playing around with my surroundings, without having to achieve anything immediate.

The pinnacle was when we found this old, intense-looking lady during the nightly aartis. She agreed to stand in a specific spot for us and gave us permission to take pictures of her with the aarti we handed her. Now, I felt comfortable enough to take an upclose portrait of her, with her aarti as a clear object of interest, and a dominant looking building in the back. Easily one of my favorite photos, with how intense the story in her eyes is, and how ethereal the framing of the shot was.

Before this experience and city, I was comfortable with taking landscapes, generic crown pictures, maybe even tripod-shots of myself. But now, collecting 200 photos while shooting for an hour in a city, lets me relive my story much more. This is, of course, what I'm noticing after the fact, but was a much needed starting point.

## Kolkata: Dialing It In

Kolkata was our final stop. Kolkata, no offense to anyone living in Kolkata, seems like an ordinary metropolitan city. Similar waterfront activity to Varanasi, similar bazaar experiences to Chennai, similar eating experiences like in Bangalore and Hyderabad. The people, here and across India tbh, are engrossed in their work, and don't care too much when we photograph. But Kolkata was the peak for my experiences and dialing in my photography skills.

The second day, I got into a fight. By choice of course. In my intention to put myself into the scene, we happened to be photographing wrestlers, and seeing my enthusiam, I gradually did their exercises, and even got into their ring. Held my ground for a couple of minutes, eventually had my butt handed to me. But I found myself liking mirroring my subjects, and entering their world, as my photography style.

We went to this gorgeously colored blue college town book bazaar area, with these generally lived in old men selling books like it was 1980. But it made for fantastic backdrops to hammer techniques, the stacks of books making the perfect leading lines, the hanging bulbs and eletric blue paint giving the most intriguing lighting. My mentor pushed me to keep getting closer and closer to people to capture their expressions as much as possible. Every time I got close, I still had to get closer. The negative space had to be cut to focus in on the one story worth telling, the people. The intention of the photo was everything.

I can't overstate how much Kolkata was useful in hammering in technique. I started travlling lighter, leaving my bag at home meant it was much less awkward for me to crouch down for those waist level shots. My friend let me try his Leica Q3 43, looking through the viewfinder and fixed focal length meant my I had to move to get my shot, leading to much more intentionality than ever before, not to mention my next birthday present for myself.

## What I Came In Knowing (and Not Knowing)

My relationship with photography has always been complicated. When I was young, I HATED even getting my photo taken. Perhaps I hated how I looked, or maybe I felt photos were useless since the moments would all come back. Parts of my thought process I have realized were significantly incorrect. Then, in my teens, specifically on a trip to Alaska, I wanted to try vlogging (thanks, Casey Neistat). Never published what I recorded, but that's when I started to enjoy getting my picture taken, and taking pictures of others. Later on trips to Europe, I remember LOVING posing my parents and taking pictures of them, and when they couldn't take good pictures of me, I took out my own tripod with a clicker to take my own pictures. This continued on all my trips over the past couple of years.

But specifically, the past year, I've noticed that my pictures weren't as good as I remembered. This one friend (grr Amruth) somehow found mistakes that I agreed with in all my pictures, perhaps the framing was off, perhaps the subject wasn't centered. And as I started meeing more creatives, tried recording my own videos, I realized there was a deeper level of story telling I had yet to unlock. This workshop came at a perfect time. I was anyway going to be in India, and a trip MEANT to broaden my horizons, while teaching me a skill that I felt I had been lacking? it felt like a sign from god.

## What I Actually Learned

I can answer that I learned how which settings to press to better expose my pictures. Or that I now know the basics of Lightroom and file management. I even got to take a stab at using a Leica. But the observational skills I learned is deeper than those mere technical details.

Immediately, I have a much deeper perspective on framing. I always preferred landscape photos, but now, I'm realizing the importance of having an eye-catching subject, and how to frame there properly. Perhaps you center them with walls, or align them with leading lines in the background. Or you add depth of story to what they're doing with adding a foreground element. And when no clear subject is visible, you can look for beams of light that automatically frame subjects for you. For example, you can narrow your focus on only their hands to highlight the action they're doing, like untangling a net. Observation underlines this ability to frame subjects well. I'm now better at observing what is happening in a moment of time. Maybe its a street vendor interaction of a person buying a book, I'd go behind the shoulder of the buyer and try to grab a picture of the vender, aligned with the stack of books behind him. And when taking these pictures of a subject, it just makes sense to get three different angles. Don't overthink one specific shot, but just go to three different sides. One top down, one close up, one wide shot. Just get everything real quick and move on.

Now that I'm placing such a large emphasis on subjects, I need to make sure the subjects feel comfortable. If my style of photography entails me going up to random people, I need to make sure I act as if I belong, and I'm not some awkward Out of place guy who's doing something wrong. But if I mirror curious energy, that would make my subjects feel comfortable. And likely to say yes to me taking pictures.

A lot of photography is being Observation about observing the world around me, and I can't just be sad at missing a previous shot. I need to keep looking forward to the next shot. I'm part of getting an extra as being able to predict what might happen. Is a person gonna walk in front of a complementary color? Is a person going to end up getting into an argument? It's just constantly being aware and even predicting what might happen. And that comes from a lot of experience.

Again, from day 1 to day 10, I'm a completely different photographer. Instead of just landscapes and pictures of me, I definitely see the value of finding the small moments around me. And now when I flip through my photo book, it's like, okay, I remember going up to all these different people. I remember going to all these different parts of the city. I'm getting better at capturing interesting moments in time. And now, whenever I see other people's pictures, I'm automatically noticing the wasted negative space, or bad framing, or how they could have lined up pictures better. I need to be careful that I'm not criticizing others, but how I can push my own work better. Not only being a tourist but being part of the scene itself.

## Photography as Practice

I'm learning that pursuing any art is a deeply introspective process. Becoming better at the art means overcoming insecurities that you're not already good at it. It means overcoming egos that you can listen to the feedback the world and the people around you are giving. It means overcoming selfishness that you want to get over being recognized for being like someone unique but just working on the art to become better at the art itself. I see parallels to the Bhagavad Gita where you have to be detached from the fruit of the labor but just keep doing the work and you keep just getting better.

I didn't notice that I was feeling flow states. There's one area where I was just doing street photography and marnaz and just circling this intersection and finding interesting people. I some for a couple minutes, forgot all my other stress of the world, and just was looking for the next cool thing to do. When you get really good at art and it just sack of nature, that's what it feels like, huh? Especially there, it's like looking at their stories is probably just as meaningful as mine. And for me to be a good artist, I have to forget about myself and observe the world around me.

And now I'm recognizing the importance of finding good inspiration for my work. Maybe I need to just expose myself to completely new fields like poetry or painting, so I can see how different people see different perspectives. And if that speaks to me, then I should adopt it.

On the opposite side, I do still struggle with my cadence of producing the work. Sometimes it's like I want to produce this one reel every single day, and that's stressing me out, and I burn out. But if I'm not improving my craft and giving myself time and space to get better, what's the point? What's the point of putting out mediocre work? I'm not sure how to answer this question yet. Maybe it's a question of getting a minimal viable work done every single day, but then when I feel super inspired, producing one really good work. When it's traveling, maybe that is reviewing my photos every single day and doing slight vlogs when it's at home, maybe doing consistent outputs every single day. During this trip, I know I felt extremely guilty for not making videos every single day, but I have to overcome guilt in my quest of becoming a better artist. That's patience for sir, sure. I know for a fact that I get very impatient with things. But especially with something like content, I need to make sure there is constant momentum. Otherwise, you fall off, people forget, and you lose the momentum. And you start overthinking again and again if your content is worth putting out.

Overall, this whole art thing is an extremely introspective and as I craft on my arc, I have to craft on my own personality.
