# My Vipassana Experience

What do you gain by meditating for 10 days, 10 hours a day, complete silence with minimal food and prison cells?

Well, this is a Vipassana meditation retreat, and straight up, it doesn't make you a different person. But it gives you a deeper insight into your thought processes. And this was my experience.

Everyone finds about Vipassana because of usually some trauma in their life, or just some deep confusion about their mind or thinking process. I know I for one suffered from I guess anxiety and severe hand tremors and Since like leaving my job even though it's been fun. It's just like there's still a sense of what is happening. What's going on? I unfortunately got into the habit of double guessing my own decisions and noticed myself in such low energy levels that you can't really focus on much else.

So doing something as outrageous as 10 days of meditation completely snaps you out of your daily routines and makes you question reality? The daily schedule was you wake up at 4:30, meditate till 8, and a lecture till 9:30. You 3 breaks between 6:30 - 8, 11:30 - 1, 5-6 for food. The more hardcore you went, you woke up earlier you took less breaks and you ate less food as the days went on I know I did I know for a fact I gave him my all by the time I got I think I lost it three four pounds you could see my face.

This is also the first time I really learned true meditation technique. It's very simple. The Palsana doesn't have you memorizing chance or visualizing, but it's very self-occurse. You start off by intensely focusing on just your respiration and focusing on the tip of your nose. For three days you do that and your sensations of your body increases tremendously. From there, you start scanning your entire body from the top of your head to the tips of your toes and back. This constant flow of attention makes you aware of your sensation throughout your body. And sometimes you feel that there is these fuzzy sensations. Sometimes you feel like this gross overall sensation where you can't really feel the small parts. These overall gross sensations are actually related to mental blocks. I'll get into this further, but the TLDR is Vipassana makes you extremely aware of the mind-muscle connection. Any bad habits or traits or nervousness or anxieties that you have in your mental state are reflected in your body. And so instead of just falling into your regular thought patterns or maybe overthinking, you slowly start to just pay attention to the corresponding sensation in your body.

This is the core philosophy of Vipassana, that the sixth sense bodies of your actual body, like taste, touch, sight, smell, sound, as well as thoughts. Vipassana treats the mind, like the conscious mind as like an input and its output as just thoughts and thoughts are just sensations. All these are reflected in your body and when you keep paying attention to the sensation, you notice that over time, sensation dulls and goes away. This is supposed to eventually make you realize that all sensations are passing and you should never get attached to a sensation. For example, just as you would not get attached to the sight of a flower, you know it dies. You should not be attached to, let's say, a thought of anger or a thought of lust.

Normally what happens is when these quote-unquote "impure thoughts" come up, we have a negative reaction because we think that we get too attached to the emotion itself because it's either a craving or an aversion. Both are a rejection of reality. Specifically, anicca, or impermance, is a concept constantly drilled into us. Having either craving or aversion forgets this concept of anicca and attaches happiness to these temporary sensations. First, an aversion itself is a craving, its a craving for the absense of whatever the opposite of the aversion is. Then, if you have a craving, you satisfy it once, you get the high, but the high fades, and you're stucking chasing it again. chasing an invisibile emotion, your happiness chained to its result.

In Vipassana, By constantly paying attention to whatever of these thoughts arise in our body, you have to be, you have to remain alert and equinanimous. Every time this craving comes, you have to be extremely aware and stop it and it strikes. Stop it as it just identifies that you are experiencing a craving. But then once you experience it, you have to be equinanimous. You have to not get dragged away by its emotional delusions and you have to not be even angry at yourself that you are experiencing these cravings because then you're going to start craving mental peace itself and that is even a bigger issue.

Now, the impact on me is, honestly, I'm not any different. I still recognize the same thought loops I go into, same bad habits, same everything. I'm not a different person at all. But yet, I'm at least a tiny bit more aware of the fact that I'm in a thought loop and that it will be passing. Like, during the meditation I did pretty good, but now when I'm back, honestly I'm struggling a lot to get even a 10% of the same depth of meditation I was doing during the vipassana. It is extremely hard. It remains to be seen if I will continue this for the next year until I get to do this again. It's pretty interesting that discipline is everything, even if you don't get results you're supposed to just keep doing it, doing it, doing it. But in a weird way this is easy and hard because easy, you kind of just have to sit there and remind yourself it's all passing. Hard because you keep getting wrapped in your own delusions, but I'd rather be aware of my delusions and just live inside of them.

It also feels like this is the hardest most important question you're supposed to face. If you're able to figure this out, the end state is you do everything for purely sense of love. This selflessness will pervade every single action you take. For example, in business, it's not about just making money for yourself, it's you truly start thinking about the other person, in your own family relationships, everything. I can sense that if I get out of my own head and embody these values, I'll become better holistically. I'll be becoming better at decision making and everything. But again, getting to that level of awareness is a lifetime of work.

Perhaps some people naturally have that, but I can't see the flaws that they have. And it makes sense to compare my spiritual journey with anyone else. For example, during the meditation, there's another guy who ate just as little as me, but he was able to stay up straight, not flinching even once. But he's also 32, so at 9 years older than me. What good would comparing myself with him do? That was his first ever vipassana as well, so was mine. Weirdly, I'm 9 years ahead of him. In that sense, even though he was at that level of discipline, or even with my own friends, there's no use of comparing my own path with anyone. Because we all just have different levels in our journey.

Overall, people, the onset sounds insane like oh 10 days of no talking, just meditation, but it flew by faster than I would ever know. I was looking forward to the Vipassana for months. It came, it was hard, and it happened in less than a blink of an eye. And I'm already planning for my next one, so I'm glad I found out about this and hopefully I continue this for the rest of my life.
